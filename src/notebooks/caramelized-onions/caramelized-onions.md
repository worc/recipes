# caramelized onions

The house method is the hybrid at the top: a short stovetop collapse in the Dutch oven, then a long descending-temperature bake on a quarter sheet. Validated on batch 02 (2026-10-09) — dark, sweet, no bitterness, ~2.5 hours mostly unattended. Everything below that is the reference material behind it.

The goal is a freezer resource, portioned small, that shows up in French onion soup, chili, beans, braises, burger toppings, and dip through the winter.

---

## the house method

**Makes ~175g finished** (about six 30g cubes) from 3 large yellow onions. Scales linearly up to the point where one quarter sheet stops holding the collapsed onions.

### what you need

| | |
|---|---|
| **Onions** | 3 large yellow, ~1200g untrimmed / ~1100g trimmed |
| **Cut** | Halved pole to pole, then food-processor mandoline at **Breville setting 2 ≈ ⅛" / 3.2mm** |
| **Fat** | Peanut oil, light hand — ~20–25g (keeps it vegan; any neutral high-smoke-point oil works) |
| **Salt** | **~3g (0.25% of raw weight). Measure this one.** See the salt trap below — it's the single easiest thing to get wrong, and the easiest to forget entirely |
| **Vessel 1** | 6qt enameled Dutch oven, on the range |
| **Vessel 2** | A single **quarter sheet pan**, heavy gauge |

### the run

1. **Stovetop collapse — ~23 min.** Onions, oil, salt into the Dutch oven over medium. Uncovered. Cook until soft, translucent, and floppy enough to fold flat — *not* until brown. Nothing browns here and nothing should.
2. **Transfer to the quarter sheet.** Scrape the pot. There will be a teaspoon or so of fond syrup; at this scale it isn't worth reducing separately, so just scrape it in with everything else.
3. **385°F, ~90 min.** Stir every 15–20 min, pulling the perimeter inward each time. The outer inch runs well ahead of the center.
4. **Step down to 350°F** once the edges start looking crisp rather than brown — around the 90 minute mark.
5. **Step down to 295°F** about 15 minutes later and cruise gently to roughly **130 min total in the oven**.
6. **Pull and cool.** Salt correction and a splash of acid at the point of use, not now — you want this neutral in the freezer.

**Total: ~2.5 hours** (23 min stovetop + 130 min oven), nearly all of it unattended.

### the descending temperature ladder

This is the part worth keeping. Start hot, step down twice.

Early on, the mass is still wet and effectively self-limiting — high heat does useful work driving water off, and nothing can scorch while there's free water around. As the onions dry and their sugars concentrate, the same temperature that was productive becomes a scorch risk, and the thin edges go first. Stepping down 385 → 350 → 295 keeps you in the browning window while backing off the part that burns. The cost is time, which on an unattended bake is the cheapest thing you have.

**Read the edges, not the clock.** Crisping at the perimeter is the signal to drop a step.

### the casserole dish variant (untested)

Worth a try next batch: run the oven phase in a ceramic or stoneware casserole instead of the quarter sheet. Lower thermal conductivity means the edges heat more gently and crisp later, and the taller walls hold a slightly deeper bed. You trade evaporation rate for evenness — expect maybe 15–30 minutes longer.

This only works because the batch is small and the Dutch oven already did the collapse. At bulk scale the evaporation penalty would matter; here it doesn't.

## what's actually happening

Two reactions, usually conflated:

- **Maillard browning** — reducing sugars (glucose, fructose) reacting with free amino acids. This is most of what you taste in a browned onion: savory, meaty, complex. Accelerates in alkaline conditions, accelerates hard with temperature.
- **Caramelization proper** — sugar pyrolysis, no nitrogen involved. Fructose starts around 230°F, sucrose closer to 320°F. Contributes, but it's the supporting act.

Raw onion is roughly **88–90% water and 4–6% sugar** by fresh weight. That water is the whole problem.

**The 212°F ceiling.** The onion mass cannot exceed the boiling point of water until the free water is gone. Everything before that point is a dehydration step wearing a browning costume. Whatever you do — baking soda, sugar, screaming heat — you are not browning meaningfully until the pan or pot has driven off the water. This is why "20 minute caramelized onions" recipes are lying and why the honest methods are all, at bottom, evaporation strategies.

Corollary: **anything that traps steam extends the pre-browning phase.** Lids, deep narrow vessels, and overloaded pans all buy you time you didn't want.

**Where the water actually goes.** It evaporates; it doesn't pool. Liquid only accumulates in the vessel when the onions express water faster than the surface can boil it off — which at household batch sizes, uncovered, basically doesn't happen. Two batches in a row produced a teaspoon of fond syrup rather than the "startling amount" that bulk recipes promise.

## variables at a glance

| Lever | Direction | Effect | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cut thickness | Thinner | More surface area, faster water loss, faster browning | Less body in the final texture; can go to paste |
| Pan/vessel width | Wider | Faster evaporation | Fewer onions per batch |
| Vessel material | Lower conductivity (ceramic, enamel) | Gentler edges, more even | Slower overall |
| Lid | On early | Collapses onions fast, releases water | Delays browning; must come off |
| Heat | Higher | Faster once water is gone | Scorch risk, uneven browning |
| **Heat, descending** | **Hot → cool across the bake** | **Fast early dry-down, no scorch late** | **Time; needs attention at the step points** |
| Salt | Early, small dose | Osmotic water release, faster collapse | Concentrates ~6x by the end — see bulk math |
| Baking soda | ~0.2% of raw mass | Raises pH, dramatic Maillard speedup | Mushy texture, soapy/metallic if overdosed |
| Sugar | Late, small dose | Color and gloss, rounds off sulfur | Cloying; burns before the onions are done if added early |
| Acid | Anytime | Slows Maillard | Add at the *end* only |
| Fat | More | Better heat transfer, prevents sticking | Greasy finished product |
| Deglazing | Often | Recovers fond, evens color | Resets the pan temp, adds time |

## the hard way (stovetop)

The baseline every other method is measured against, and still the right call for a single pan's worth. Wide skillet, 12" minimum, stainless or cast iron. Roughly **450g raw onions per 12" pan** — more than that and you're steaming.

1. Halve pole to pole, then slice with the grain (root to tip) about 3mm. With-the-grain slices hold their shape; against-the-grain collapses to jam faster.
2. Fat: **15–20g per 450g raw**. Butter browns its own milk solids and contributes to the Maillard, but the solids scorch late in the cook. Half butter, half neutral oil is the compromise. All ghee if you want butter flavor without the scorch.
3. Medium heat. Salt lightly.
4. **Sweat phase, 10–15 min.** Onions go translucent and collapse. Nothing is browning yet and nothing can.
5. **Dry-down phase, 15–25 min.** Liquid boils off. Pan is loud, then quieter. This is the transition.
6. **Browning phase, 25–45 min.** Reduce to medium-low. Fond appears on the pan bottom; deglaze with a splash of water or stock every time it threatens to go dark, scrape, stir back in. That deglaze-and-reincorporate cycle is where most of the even color comes from.
7. Finish: acid off-heat. Sherry vinegar, balsamic, a splash of wine. Salt correction.

**Honest total: 45–75 minutes.** 90+ for the near-black jammy end.

**Why this still goes darker than the oven.** An open skillet browns by conduction against metal well above ambient air temperature. A 325°F oven can't match that intensity no matter how long it runs. The oven buys uniformity, scale, and freedom from the spatula; the skillet buys depth. To get both: oven batch, then finish in cast iron in two or three shifts.

## scaling up (true bulk)

Above roughly 2.5kg the house method stops fitting on one quarter sheet, and some things change:

- **Use a roasting pan or two half sheets**, bed no deeper than 4cm. An enameled Dutch oven alone is the reflexive pick for bulk and it's the wrong one — tall walls trap steam and stretch the dry-down.
- **A covered opening phase becomes worth it.** 400°F, lid on, 45–60 min, to collapse the mass before spreading it.
- **The drain-and-reduce step finally pays off.** At bulk scale there genuinely is pooled liquid; reserve it, reduce to a syrup separately, and add it back **at the very end** — pouring it in early re-wets the mass and puts you back under the 212°F ceiling.
- **Total: 2.5–3.5 hours for 3–5kg**, mostly unattended.

**Notes on enamel:** fond sticks to enamel and will stain it. Deglaze at the stir and again during any finish. Bar Keepers Friend gets the staining out. Don't scrape enamel with metal.

**Slow cooker variant:** 8–10 hours on low. Produces a lot of liquid and a texture that's more stewed than browned, because it never gets near browning temperature — you end up doing the actual browning in a pan afterward anyway. Skip it.

## chemical accelerants

### baking soda

The real one. Raises onion pH from ~5.5 to neutral-or-above, and Maillard rate climbs steeply with pH. Visible browning arrives dramatically sooner.

- **Dose: 1g per 500g raw onions** (~¼ tsp per pound). This is a ceiling, not a target. Start at half that.
- **Cost: pectin degrades in alkaline conditions.** The onions go soft, then mushy, then paste. There is no version of this where you keep the texture.
- Overdose tastes soapy and metallic, and the color goes drab brown rather than mahogany.
- **Verdict:** good when the destination is a purée or soup base where texture doesn't survive anyway. Bad for anything going on a burger. For a versatile freezer batch, leave it out — you can add it to a specific application later, but you can't put the texture back.

### pressure cooker

15 psi puts you at ~250°F, above the water ceiling, in a sealed environment. Onions + fat + a pinch of baking soda, 20 minutes at pressure, then reduce the resulting liquid. Fast and genuinely brown. Same texture cost as baking soda, amplified. A soup-base tool.

### the free ones

- **Salt early** — osmosis pulls water out of the cells so it can evaporate instead of steaming from inside.
- **Cut finer** — surface area is free speed.
- **Deglaze aggressively** — the fond on the pan is browner than the onions are; putting it back in is how you get color without pushing the whole mass harder.
- **Don't add acid until the end.** Wine, vinegar, or tomato early will measurably slow Maillard.

## added sugar

Onions bring their own 4–6%. Adding more does three things, only one of which is "faster."

- **Color and gloss** arrive sooner because added sucrose caramelizes on its own schedule, independent of the Maillard. This can fool you into pulling early — it *looks* done before the savory development has happened.
- **Rounds off residual sulfur harshness**, a real benefit with white onions or a rushed batch.
- **Burns.** Added sugar hits its scorch point well before the onions finish. If you use it, add it in the last 10–15 minutes.

**Dose if you use it: 4g (1 tsp) per 450g raw.** Brown sugar brings molasses, its own flavor decision. Honey scorches faster than sucrose. Balsamic at the finish gives you sugar and acid together and is the best version of this idea.

**Verdict:** unnecessary for yellow onions cooked properly. A correction, not a shortcut.

## allium comparison

| Allium | Water/sugar | Time vs. yellow | Finished character | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Yellow** | Balanced, ~5% sugar, moderate sulfur | baseline | Mahogany, savory-sweet, holds some texture | The default for good reason. Best flavor-per-dollar for bulk. |
| **Sweet (Vidalia, Walla Walla, Maui)** | Higher water, *similar* sugar, low sulfur | +20–30% | Soft, one-note sweet, less depth | The "sweet" name is about low pyruvate, not high sugar. More water to drive off, less sulfur to convert into complexity. Paying more for a longer cook and a flatter result. |
| **White** | Lower sugar, sharp sulfur | −10% | Cleaner, sharper, less complex | Browns a touch faster. Benefits more than others from a pinch of sugar at the end. |
| **Red** | Slightly higher sugar, anthocyanins | baseline | Good flavor, **ugly color** | Anthocyanins go gray-brown-blue at neutral pH. Absolutely no baking soda. A splash of acid preserves some red. Fine in a mixed batch, poor as the headline. |
| **Shallot** | Low water, high sugar | −30–40% | Deep, refined, almost fruity | The luxury option. Caramelizes fast and beautifully, goes bitter if pushed past the yellow-onion endpoint. Expensive at bulk scale. A small shallot batch alongside a big yellow batch is a good use of a grill day. |
| **Leek (whites & light greens)** | Low sulfur, delicate | −20% | Melts rather than browns; silky, mild | Never gets to mahogany. A different product, not a cheaper onion. Wash obsessively — grit is the failure mode. |
| **Cipollini** | High sugar, small | varies | Excellent whole-glazed | Better braised whole than sliced. |
| **Scallion / spring onion** | Mostly water and greens | n/a | Not worth it sliced | Char the whites whole instead. |
| **Garlic** | n/a | burns at ~⅓ the time | Bitter if cooked alongside | Add in the last 10 minutes, or roast separately and fold in. Roasted garlic + caramelized onion in the same freezer cube is a strong move. |

**Mixed-allium batch:** works, but cook by cohort. Yellows first, shallots at the halfway point, garlic in the last 10 minutes.

## bulk math

**Yield: 15–20% of raw weight, and where you land tells you how dark you went.**

| Endpoint | Yield | Measured |
|---|---|---|
| Golden brown | ~20% | Batch 01 — 1450g → ~275g |
| Dark, jammy | **~15%** | Batch 02 — ~1100g → **173g** |

Darker costs yield, because the extra color comes from driving off more water. If a batch comes in high, it's paler than you think it is.

**Planning numbers:** 1kg raw → 150–200g finished. A 5-pound (2270g) bag → **350–450g**, about 12–15 one-ounce portions.

**Packing density is roughly 0.5 g/ml** loose — batch 02's 173g filled about 12 fl oz. Useful for guessing container sizes before you weigh anything.

**The salt trap.** Salt raw onions at 1% by mass — which feels normal — and you end up with ~6% salt in the finished product. Inedible, and worse, unusable as an ingredient, because every dish it enters is now pre-salted at an unknown level.

> **Salt at 0.2–0.3% of raw weight** (~3g for 1200g; 5–7g for a 5lb bag). Enough for the osmotic effect, low enough that the finished onions land near 1.5% and behave like a neutral ingredient. Correct salt at the point of use, not in the freezer container.

**Fat scales differently.** Stovetop wants 15–20g per 450g raw because the pan is the only heat source. In the oven, heat on all sides, **20–25g per kilogram** is plenty. Over-fatted freezer onions release a slick when thawed.

**Don't fill past ⅔** of any covered vessel during the collapse phase. The volume drop is dramatic but it happens *after* the moment it would have boiled over.

## storage & freezing

**Ice cube tray, same as the garlic butter.** Portion at 30g (1 oz) per cube, freeze solid, pop out, vacuum-seal or double-bag. One cube is a single-serving flavor hit; three or four is the onion base for a pot of chili.

- **Freezer: 3–6 months** for quality, indefinite for safety. Label with date, allium type, and whether baking soda was used.
- **Fridge: 4–5 days.** Longer if finished with vinegar.
- **Flat-pack alternative:** vacuum bag, rolled flat, score-lined so you can snap off portions. Better freezer real estate than cubes above a kilo or so.

**Safety note worth saying out loud:** onions and garlic in oil at room temperature are a *C. botulinum* risk — low-acid, anaerobic, exactly the conditions it likes. Caramelized onions stored under a layer of oil must live in the fridge or freezer, never the counter. And there is **no USDA-tested process for home-canning caramelized onions**, water bath or pressure. Freeze them. Onion jam recipes calling for water-bath canning are relying on an acid level that varies batch to batch.

## troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| An hour in, still pale and watery | Overloaded pan or trapped steam | Split across two pans, or move to a wider vessel uncovered. Still in dry-down. |
| Pale with two or three browned stragglers | Still in dry-down — the stragglers are the pieces that got above the liquid line | Nothing is wrong. Keep going. |
| Brown but tastes thin | Pulled before the Maillard developed | Keep going. Color from added sugar arrives before flavor does. |
| Edges crisping while the middle is still soft | Oven too hot for how dry the mass now is | **Step the temperature down.** This is the ladder's whole purpose. Stir the perimeter inward. |
| A few scorched specks at the far pan edges | Normal — the corners of a sheet pan run hottest and a stray slice or two never gets mixed in | Leave them behind when you pack. Not a batch failure. |
| Mushy paste, no structure | Baking soda, or sliced against the grain, or both | Not recoverable as texture. Reframe as soup base. |
| Soapy or metallic | Baking soda overdose | Halve the dose next batch. A little acid at the finish masks some of it. |
| Bitter | Garlic added too early, or shallots overcooked | Add garlic in the last 10 min. Pull shallots earlier than yellows. |
| Gray-blue tinge | Red onions at neutral/alkaline pH | Cosmetic only. Add acid earlier, or accept it, or skip reds. |
| Greasy when thawed | Too much fat for the oven method | Drain before freezing. Reduce to ~20g/kg next batch. |
| Yield came in above 20% | Paler than intended | Not a problem, just know what you have. More water is still in there. |

**Log-keeping notes:**
- Weigh raw into a tared bowl before slicing. Everything else is a ratio hanging off that number.
- Weigh finished before portioning — yield % is the most transferable figure in the table, and now that there are two points it reads as a darkness gauge, not just a bookkeeping line.
- **Record the mandoline setting**, with its measured equivalent. Breville ~2 came in at ⅛" / 3.2mm, right on the 3mm target from the stovetop method.
- Count-up timing, not countdown. Record what the onions actually took.
- Note the temperature step points by clock time — the ladder is the method's signature and the step timing is what transfers.
- Holding onion variety constant is what makes two batches comparable.

## accumulated findings

- **The sheet pan phase is the whole cook.** Batch 01: 115 of 165 minutes. Batch 02: 130 of 153. The collapse stage is short regardless of how you do it.
- **Stage one is a collapse step, not a browning step.** Its job is soft, translucent, foldable. On the range that took 23 minutes; in a 400°F oven it took 50. The range wins and it isn't close — direct conduction plus an open top beats ambient air.
- **Small batches don't generate reservable liquid — confirmed twice, two ways.** Oven at 1450g and range at 1100g both yielded about a teaspoon. Pooling needs the onions to express water faster than the surface boils it off, which doesn't happen uncovered at this scale. Drain-and-reduce is a bulk technique; don't plan on it below ~2500g.
- **Descending temperature is the fix for crisp edges.** Not lower temperature throughout — that just makes everything slower. High early where there's still water to protect the sugars, stepping down as the mass dries.
- **Yield is a darkness gauge.** ~20% golden, ~15% dark. Two points, consistent with the mechanism.
- **Verify the oven.** Set to 360°F, measured 325°F at the door after recovery. A 35°F deficit is a time cost, not a quality problem — but worth knowing rather than mistaking a slow oven for stalled onions.
- **Raw volume is a bad guide to vessel capacity.** 1450g looked like a full 6qt and collapsed to a third within fifteen minutes.
- **Salt is the one measurement that can't be eyeballed later.** A light hand got away with it on batch 02, but it's unrecoverable if overdone and it's the thing most likely to be forgotten in the shuffle of prep.
